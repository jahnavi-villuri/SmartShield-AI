## Intended use
Show a learner how a text classifier flags unwanted SMS (promotional or scam) and which words drove the score. Two stages: a main model separates ordinary from unwanted messages, then a small second model separates promotional from scam.

**Out of scope:** Blocking, deleting or reporting messages automatically; judging non-English messages; any decision about a person; replacing a carrier's spam filter.

## Training data
UCI SMS Spam Collection, Mendeley SMS Phishing Dataset, ScamShield `Smishing_Dataset` (English, non-synthetic rows), 3,000 template-written bank/transaction messages, and the owner's own real messages (hash-split, never evaluated on training rows).

Sizes: train 55,258, val 11,164, test 11,152, external 2,049, challenge 33. Details: `docs/dataset_cards/`.

Threshold 0.70 chosen on validation data (highest validation recall with validation precision >= 0.98). The external set comes from a different region and period; its drop is the honest estimate of how the model behaves on messages unlike its training data. The spam share in training and test data (38%) is far higher than in a real phone, so real-world precision will be lower than shown. On the owner's own held-out messages, 2 of 8 legitimate messages were wrongly flagged (13 in total).

**Second stage (promotional vs scam):** `logreg_word_norm` trained on 380 hand-labelled messages (123 scams). Cross-validated: precision 0.91, recall 0.85, accuracy 0.92. Its labels were produced by one AI labeller and are not human-verified.

## Known limitations
- Trained on data from about 2011-2021; no 2025-2026 data was available.
- Training spam share is ~38%, far above real inboxes: real-world precision will be lower.
- Ham and spam in the training sources differ by region and style; part of the score may reflect source.
- Legitimate bank alerts were flagged as spam until synthetic template examples were added; a 33-message hand-written challenge set still shows 4 of 23 legitimate messages flagged.
- Conversational and impersonation scams (e.g. 'Mum, I lost my phone') are largely missed.

## Ethical considerations
A bank alert wrongly marked as scam can hide something a person needs to see, which is why the threshold favours precision. The data over-represents UK/US and Indian text and formal bank wording; messages in other languages or styles are treated unreliably. The app never says a message is 'safe'.
